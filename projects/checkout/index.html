01 / The question

Find friction before proposing a solution.

In e-commerce, completing a task is only part of the experience. A task may be technically successful while still requiring extra effort. I wanted to investigate whether behavioural indicators such as time and errors could help identify where a research team should look more closely.

My role was to analyse an existing educational dataset, choose suitable statistical tests, interpret results and translate them into hypotheses for future research. I did not design the simulated test or collect the data.

Where does performance vary?

Compare task success across task and device categories.

What accompanies failure?

Compare recorded duration and errors for successful and failed tasks.

How does performance feel?

Explore the relationships between errors, frustration and satisfaction.

What should be tested next?

Translate patterns into research priorities rather than assumed design defects.

02 / Research approach

From raw observations to usable evidence.

I used the medium version of Dataset 2 from Mohsen Rafiei's UX Datasets Collection (2025). It simulates four tasks: account setup, product search, checkout and payment, across desktop, mobile and tablet. The 200 rows contain 11 variables. The 197 non-missing participant IDs are unique; three IDs are missing, so independence is assumed rather than fully verified.

01 · Screen the dataset

Checked missing values, variable types, distributions and possible outliers. Kept the recorded values rather than replacing missing data. The saved analyses use available cases per test.

02 · Choose tests for the measurements

Used chi-square for categorical associations, Mann-Whitney U for skewed time and count outcomes, Spearman correlation for ordinal measures and a binomial logistic model for task and device categories.

03 · Check assumptions and interpret

Completion time and error count were right-skewed (Shapiro-Wilk p < .001 for both). Expected counts exceeded five in both contingency tables. Potential outliers were retained because extreme values may represent friction in the simulated scenario.

The analysis is exploratory; no hypotheses were preregistered. The tests use different valid sample sizes. Reported p-values are unadjusted.

03 / Findings

Errors stand out more than task or device type.

The strongest pattern is that failed tasks contain more errors and higher error counts are associated with greater frustration.

Failed tasks also had a median of two errors, compared with zero for successful tasks. This makes error prevention and recovery a useful starting point for future usability investigation.

Neither errors nor frustration showed a statistically significant association with satisfaction. A single satisfaction score may therefore tell a different story from a more specific measure of friction in this dataset.

Time is a potential signal, not an explanation. A failed task's recorded duration is not necessarily time to completion. Future testing should distinguish time spent attempting a task from time to successful completion.

Payment and checkout had lower descriptive success rates, but the task-level association was not statistically significant. Device-level success was also similar (desktop 80.2%, mobile 84.4%, tablet 80.0%; p = .813). The logistic model combining task and device was not significant (p = .466). There is insufficient evidence to target one device or task exclusively.

04 / Research to action

Prioritise error prevention, then validate.

These are proposed research and design directions for a hypothetical product team, not implemented changes or measured improvements.

Priority 01

Investigate error prevention and recovery

Run moderated tests of representative checkout and account tasks. Observe navigation and validation problems, then test clearer instructions, inline validation and recoverable error states if those problems are confirmed.

Measure: task success, errors per task and recovery success.

Priority 02

Capture frustration alongside performance

Add a short post-task frustration question and ask users what made the task difficult. Compare observed errors with their explanations rather than assuming the source of frustration.

Measure: frustration ratings, task success and qualitative feedback.

Priority 03

Investigate payment and checkout across devices

Use the lower descriptive success rates as a starting hypothesis. Recruit users across device types, observe specific breakdowns and compare revised flows against a baseline before prioritising device-specific changes.

Measure: success rate, time to successful completion and abandonment.

05 / Reflection

What the analysis can and cannot tell us.

The dataset is synthetic and includes built-in statistical relationships, including those between task failure, errors and frustration. The patterns demonstrate an analysis workflow, not independent empirical evidence of real user behaviour. There is no actual interface, session recording or qualitative account to explain the observed patterns.

Some variables are missing. Sample sizes differ between tests, some groups are small and the regression has few failures relative to its five predictors. The multiple exploratory tests increase the risk of false positives. The time result is less robust than the two p < .001 findings, and none of these p-values establish real-world effects.

If I were conducting this study with a product team, I would define primary outcomes in advance, recruit real users across devices, combine behavioural measures with qualitative observations and validate any proposed changes in a follow-up test. No product or business impact is claimed here.
